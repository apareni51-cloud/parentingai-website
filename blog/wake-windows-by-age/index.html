What Are Wake Windows by Age, and Are the Charts Real?

By the ParentingAI team · Last updated August 2026

Wake windows are how long a baby stays awake between sleeps. The chart below reflects what sleep consultants and parenting communities use — no medical body publishes wake-window numbers. What is published is total sleep per 24 hours: the AASM, endorsed by the AAP, says 12–16 hours for 4–12 months.

That is the honest version, and you deserve it before the chart, not after. Wake windows are a useful organizing idea that a lot of families find helpful. They are not a clinical standard, and treating them like one is where the 3am spiralling starts.

Where do wake window numbers come from?

From sleep consultants, baby sleep apps and parenting forums — refined over years of practice and passed between parents. They are practitioner heuristics, not published guidance.

Dr. Craig Canapari, a pediatric sleep physician who directs the Sleep Medicine Program at Yale School of Medicine, puts it plainly: wake windows are "not a concept that is taught, discussed, or researched in medical school or in the world of pediatric sleep medicine," and "a PubMed search pulled up zero references on the term." He is not dismissive about it — his view is that "often, any plan beats no plan" — but he also tells parents they should not feel obliged to use them, and recommends watching for signs of sleepiness instead.

Evidence note: No wake-window chart anywhere on the internet, including this one, is derived from published pediatric sleep research. The AAP, the CDC, the WHO and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine do not publish wake-window durations. The numbers below are a synthesis of the ranges in common use among sleep consultants and parenting communities. We publish them because they are genuinely useful as a starting point, and we label them because you should know what you are working with.

What are the typical wake windows by age?

Age Wake window (commonly used) Naps per day
0–4 weeks 35–60 min 6–8
4–8 weeks 45–75 min 5–6
2–3 months 1–1.5 hours 4–5
4–5 months 1.5–2.5 hours 3–4
6–7 months 2–3 hours 3
8–9 months 2.5–3.5 hours 2–3
10–12 months 3–4 hours 2
13–17 months 4–5.5 hours 1–2
18+ months 5–6 hours 1

Use these as a starting hypothesis for a baby whose cues you cannot read yet — not as a schedule to defend. If your 5-month-old is happy for three hours and naps beautifully, your baby is right and the chart is wrong.

How much sleep does a baby actually need?

This is the question with a real answer behind it. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine published consensus recommendations on sleep duration for children, and the American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed them in June 2016. Both are talking about total sleep per 24 hours, naps included:

Age Recommended sleep per 24 hours
4–12 months 12 to 16 hours (including naps)
1–2 years 11 to 14 hours (including naps)
3–5 years 10 to 13 hours (including naps)
6–12 years 9 to 12 hours

If your baby is landing inside their band across the day, the shape of how they got there matters far less than parenting content suggests. A baby who takes three short naps and sleeps ten hours at night, and a baby who takes two long naps and sleeps nine, can both be doing fine.

How do I read my baby's tired cues?

This is the part with the strongest footing, and it deserves more of your attention than the chart does. A clock cannot see your baby. You can.

Early cues — this is your window:

  • Yawning
  • Rubbing eyes or face
  • Staring into the middle distance, losing focus
  • Going quiet, losing interest in a toy they liked a minute ago
  • Turning away from faces and stimulation
  • Getting clingy

Move toward sleep at the first of these, not the third. Once you see them, you generally have a few minutes before the calm window closes.

Late cues — you have gone past it:

  • Wired, frantic energy that looks like a second wind
  • Arching the back
  • Crying that is hard to interrupt
  • Fighting sleep while visibly exhausted
  • Naps that end at 25 minutes

If you are consistently seeing the second list, shortening the awake stretch by 15 minutes for a few days is a low-cost experiment. That is the practical value of wake windows: a place to start adjusting from.

Do wake windows change?

Constantly, especially in the first year. A stretch that worked two weeks ago can stop working without warning, usually around a nap transition or a developmental leap. The first wake window of the day is often the shortest — many babies handle less awake time in the morning than they do before bed. If the first nap is a daily battle, trying it 15 minutes earlier is a reasonable place to start.

The bottom line

Wake windows are a helpful framework with no medical body behind them. Total sleep across 24 hours is the number that is actually published — 12 to 16 hours for babies 4 to 12 months, per the AASM and endorsed by the AAP. Watch your baby's cues first, use the chart as a backup when the cues are unclear, and hold both loosely.
