When Is a Baby's Fever an Emergency?

By the ParentingAI team · Last updated August 2026

If your baby is under 3 months old and has a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, go to the emergency room now. Not a message through the portal. Not a call in the morning. For older babies, the number matters less than how they look and act.

🚨 The one rule that has no exceptions

Under 3 months old + rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher = go to the ER immediately.

Not a phone call in the morning. Not "let's see how she does overnight." Go.

Take the temperature again if you want to be certain of the reading. Then get in the car, or call 911 if your baby is struggling to breathe, is limp, or will not wake up.

Here is why that rule is so absolute. A newborn's immune system is brand new. A serious bacterial infection — in the blood, the urine, or the lining of the brain — can look exactly like a mild cold in the first hours, and then move very fast. Newborns also do not reliably get the other signals we rely on in older children. Sometimes the fever is the only thing there is.

So the medical world does not gamble on it. The American Academy of Pediatrics has a full clinical practice guideline devoted to nothing but fever in infants 8 to 60 days old, and it defines fever as a temperature at or above 100.4°F (38°C). Emergency departments have a standing workup for these babies. You are not overreacting. You are doing the thing the guideline was written for.

One more piece, from the AAP's own symptom guidance: for a baby under 12 weeks, do not give any fever-reducing medicine before your baby is evaluated. Bringing the temperature down can mask the picture the clinical team needs to see.

What temperature counts as a fever in a baby?

100.4°F (38°C) or higher, taken rectally. That is the line the American Academy of Pediatrics draws, and it is the same line in every age group.

What changes with age is not the definition of fever. What changes is what you do about it.

What are the fever thresholds by age?

These are the AAP's guidance points for when to make contact. Read them as "pick up the phone at this number," not "everything below this number is fine" — a baby who looks unwell gets a call regardless of what the thermometer says.

Your child's age Temperature that means act What to do
Under 3 months 100.4°F (38°C) rectal Emergency room, immediately. No exceptions.
3–6 months 101°F (38.3°C) Call your pediatrician. The AAP flags this age band for prompt contact, and the AAP symptom guidance says babies 3–6 months with fever should be discussed with a doctor within 24 hours.
6–24 months 103°F (39.4°C) Call your pediatrician. Also call if a fever in a 6–12 month old lasts more than 48 hours with no other symptoms.
Over 2 years Behavior matters more than the number Call if the fever lasts more than 3 days, goes above 104°F (40°C), returns after being gone more than 24 hours, or your child seems genuinely unwell.

Two clarifications that trip parents up.

First: the number in the "3–6 months" row does not cancel the row above it. On the day your baby turns 3 months, the rule above them still deserves respect — a fever in a 12-week-old and a fever in a 13-week-old are not different events, and the AAP's own infant fever guideline runs to 60 days with a cautious approach out to 12 weeks. If your baby is anywhere near that boundary and running a fever, call.

Second: a fever without any obvious cause is treated more carefully than a fever with a clear one. Your pediatrician will want to know what else is going on.

How do I take my baby's temperature accurately?

Rectally, for any baby under 3 months. The American Academy of Pediatrics is direct about this: rectal is "the most reliable and accurate for infants, especially babies under 3 months old."

Nobody enjoys this. It is also over in thirty seconds, and it is the reading that a nurse on the phone at 2am will actually act on.

How to do it:

  1. Put a small amount of petroleum jelly on the tip of a digital thermometer.
  2. Lay your baby belly-down across your lap, or on their back with their legs pulled up toward their chest.
  3. Slide the thermometer gently into the anus — about ½ inch for a baby under 6 months, up to 1 inch for an older baby. Stop if you feel resistance.
  4. Hold your baby still until it beeps. Read it.
  5. Clean the thermometer with soap and water or an alcohol wipe, before and after.

Which thermometer for which age, per the AAP:

  • Rectal — the standard for under 3 months, and usable at any age.
  • Ear (tympanic) — 6 months and older. Younger babies have ear canals too narrow for a reliable reading.
  • Forehead (temporal artery) — can be used at any age.
  • Armpit (axillary) — the least accurate of the options. Useful as a rough screen, not as the reading you make a decision on. If an armpit reading looks high in a young baby, confirm it rectally.
  • Oral — once your child is about 4 years old.

Do not use a glass mercury thermometer. The AAP is explicit: they can break and release toxic mercury fumes. If you have one in the house, your local hazardous waste program will take it.

And do not diagnose a fever by touch. The AAP specifically says not to rely on feeling your baby's forehead or skin, and not to use temperature-sensitive "fever strips." Hands are terrible thermometers, and a baby who has been bundled in a car seat feels warm no matter what.

Is the fever itself dangerous?

Almost never. The fever is the response, not the disease.

This is the single most useful reframe in this whole article. As the AAP puts it: when the body detects an infection, the brain deliberately raises body temperature to help fight it. Fever is your baby's immune system switching on.

Which means two things follow, and they surprise most parents:

A higher number does not mean a sicker child. A toddler with a viral cold can hit 103°F and be running around the living room. A newborn with a serious bacterial infection can sit at 100.5°F. The temperature tells you the immune system is engaged. It does not tell you what it is engaged against.

Bringing the number down does not treat anything. Fever reducers make a child more comfortable. They do not shorten the illness or fight the infection. That is not a reason to skip them — a comfortable child drinks more, sleeps better, and recovers more easily — but it does mean the goal is a comfortable child, not a specific number on a thermometer.

So: watch the child, not the thermometer. Which brings us to the part that actually matters most.

What symptoms matter more than the number?

These. Every one of these earns a call or a trip in, whatever the thermometer says. The AAP's own symptom guidance sorts them roughly like this.

Call 911 now: - Severe trouble breathing — struggling for each breath, can barely cry or speak - Not moving, or you cannot wake them up - Purple or blood-colored spots or dots on the skin - Your gut says this is a life-threatening emergency

Go to the ER now: - Any fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months - A seizure with the fever - A stiff neck — cannot move the neck normally - The soft spot on top of the head looks swollen or bulging - Hard to wake, or "out of it" when awake

Call your doctor now: - Trouble breathing, but not severe - Real difficulty swallowing fluids or their own spit - A temperature above 104°F (40°C) at any age - Shaking chills lasting more than 30 minutes - Nonstop crying, or crying whenever they are touched or moved - Not moving an arm or a leg normally - Signs of dehydration - Any child with a weakened immune system

About the rash. A rash that does not fade when you press on it — press the side of a clear drinking glass against it and the spots stay visible through the glass — is the one to take seriously. The CDC lists a dark purple rash among the signs of meningococcal bloodstream infection, and notes that in babies the illness can also show up as poor feeding, unusual sleepiness, irritability, vomiting and a bulging soft spot, and that symptoms can worsen rapidly after starting out looking like flu. This is rare. It is also the reason "call now" exists.

About dehydration. The AAP's signs in a baby: fewer than six wet diapers a day, a dry or parched mouth, fewer tears when crying, a sunken soft spot, playing less than usual. In more serious dehydration: very fussy or excessively sleepy, sunken eyes, cool or discolored hands and feet, wrinkled skin, urinating only once or twice a day. Fever increases fluid loss, so this is worth checking every few hours during any feverish illness.

About inconsolability. A baby who cries no matter what you do, or who has gone quiet and floppy when they are usually alert, is telling you something the thermometer cannot. Both ends of that — inconsolable and unrousable — are reasons to be seen.

What is a febrile seizure, and is it dangerous?

Almost always, no — and it will be one of the most frightening things you ever watch. Both of those are true at once, and you deserve to hear them together.

A febrile seizure is a seizure triggered by a fever, usually as the temperature is rising quickly. The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke describes what it looks like: loss of consciousness, uncontrollable shaking, jerking or stiffening, eyes rolling back, and loss of bladder or bowel control.

Here is what the research says. Febrile seizures occur in about 2% to 5% of children in the US and Europe, peaking between 12 and 18 months, and most often affecting children between 6 months and 5 years. A simple febrile seizure — a single episode lasting 15 minutes or less within a 24-hour period — is, in the clinical literature's own language, typically harmless and does not cause long-term neurological or cognitive problems. A single episode is not linked to learning disabilities, lower intelligence, behavioral problems, or executive functioning difficulties.

About 1% to 2% of children who have simple febrile seizures go on to develop epilepsy, compared with 0.5% to 0.9% of children who never had one. NINDS puts it plainly: having a febrile seizure does not mean a child has epilepsy.

What to do while it is happening, per NINDS:

  • Note the time it starts. You will be asked. Set a timer if you can.
  • Turn your child onto their side or stomach so saliva can drain and they do not choke.
  • Put nothing in their mouth. Not a finger, not a spoon. They cannot swallow their tongue.
  • Do not restrain them. Move furniture and hard objects out of the way instead.
  • Stay with them. That is the whole job.

Call 911 if the seizure lasts longer than five minutes, or if your child does not recover quickly after a shorter one. Also call 911 for any seizure accompanied by trouble breathing.

And call your pediatrician after any first febrile seizure, even a short one that ended on its own — they will want to see your child and identify the source of the fever.

Now the honest part. Knowing all of this will not stop you from feeling like the floor dropped out. Parents describe febrile seizures as the worst moment of their lives, and then the child wakes up groggy and confused and is fine by dinner. If it happens to you, the shaking-hands feeling afterward is normal, and it is worth telling your pediatrician about that too.

When do I call, and when do I go in?

Go in — ER or 911 — for: any fever at all in a baby under 3 months; severe breathing trouble; a child who will not wake; a seizure; a non-blanching purple rash; a stiff neck or bulging soft spot.

Call your pediatrician for: everything on the "call your doctor now" list above; a fever above 104°F (40°C); a fever lasting more than 3 days at any age; a fever that goes away and returns after more than 24 hours; a baby 3–6 months at 101°F or higher; a 6–24 month old at 103°F or higher; signs of dehydration; or any time your instinct says something is off.

Two practical things to do today, before you need them.

Put your pediatrician's after-hours number in your phone under a name you can find at 3am. Most practices have a nurse line, and nurses answer this exact question all night long — it is a large part of the job, and there is no such thing as calling too soon about a young baby's fever.

And put a working digital rectal thermometer where you can find it in the dark. The night you need it is not the night to discover the battery is dead.

What should I NOT do when my baby has a fever?

  • Do not give any fever medicine to a baby under 12 weeks before they have been evaluated. The AAP flags this specifically. It can mask what the clinical team needs to see.
  • Do not give aspirin to a child or teenager with a viral illness. The AAP is unambiguous — aspirin is linked to Reye syndrome, a rare but serious condition affecting the brain and liver. This applies to anything containing aspirin or salicylates, so check labels on combination products.
  • Do not use alcohol baths, ice packs, or cold sponging. The AAP states these are no longer recommended and can actually have adverse effects.
  • Do not bundle a feverish child in blankets. A single layer of clothing is the AAP's guidance. Trapping heat works against what the body is trying to manage.
  • Do not decide anything from an armpit reading in a young baby. Confirm rectally before you act on it.
  • Do not go by touch, and do not use fever strips. Both are unreliable, per the AAP.
  • Do not wake a comfortably sleeping child to medicate a number. The goal is a comfortable child, not a specific reading. If you are unsure, ask your pediatrician what they want you to do overnight.

On medication generally: which fever reducer is appropriate for your child, whether they need one at all, and what the correct dose is are questions for your pediatrician. Doses for infants and toddlers are based on weight, not age, and the difference between a 15-pound and a 25-pound one-year-old is a real difference. Ask your pediatrician for the number that matches your child's current weight, write it down, and confirm it again at each well visit as they grow.

The bottom line

Under 3 months and 100.4°F rectally — you go. That one is not a judgment call, and you never have to spend a second wondering whether you are overreacting.

For every age after that, the fever is a signal that your child's immune system is doing its job, and the more useful information is in how your child looks, drinks, breathes and responds to you. Trust that. Pediatric nurse lines exist because parents call them, constantly, about exactly this.

And if you are reading this at 3am with a warm baby on your chest and your phone at 6% — you are already doing the thing that keeps children safe, which is paying attention.
