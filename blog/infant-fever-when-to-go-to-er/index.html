When Is a Baby's Fever an Emergency? What the American Academy of Pediatrics Says

By the ParentingAI team · Last updated August 2026

The American Academy of Pediatrics draws one line with no exceptions: a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months old means emergency care, immediately. For older babies, the Academy's guidance weighs how a child looks and acts more heavily than the number.

One thing before anything else — it is what every pediatrician's after-hours line says at the start of every call, and it applies to this article too: if you think your child is having an emergency, stop reading and call 911. No article, and no phone queue, comes before that.

This article summarizes published guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the National Institutes of Health (NIH). It is not medical advice. Your pediatrician is the person to call about your child.

The one rule the American Academy of Pediatrics has no exceptions for

Per the American Academy of Pediatrics: under 3 months old + rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher = emergency care immediately.

Not a portal message. Not "let's see how she does overnight." The Academy's guidance is to be seen now — and if your baby is struggling to breathe, is limp, or will not wake up, that is a 911 call.

Why is the guidance so absolute? The American Academy of Pediatrics explains that a newborn's immune system is brand new: a serious bacterial infection can look like a mild cold in its first hours and move fast, and in newborns the fever is sometimes the only sign there is. The Academy maintains an entire clinical practice guideline on nothing but fever in infants 8 to 60 days old, and emergency departments have a standing workup for these babies. In other words: showing up with a feverish newborn is not overreacting — it is what the guideline was written for.

One more piece of American Academy of Pediatrics guidance for this age: for a baby under 12 weeks, the Academy says not to give fever-reducing medicine before the baby has been evaluated, because lowering the temperature can mask what the clinical team needs to see.

What temperature counts as a fever?

100.4°F (38°C) or higher. That is where the American Academy of Pediatrics draws the line, at every age. What changes with age, in their guidance, is not the definition — it is what to do about it.

The American Academy of Pediatrics also says the reading that counts for a young baby is a rectal one: they call it "the most reliable and accurate for infants, especially babies under 3 months old." Their guidance is not to act on touch, fever strips, or (in young babies) an unconfirmed armpit reading — and their step-by-step instructions for taking a temperature at every age are on their parent site, healthychildren.org. Two of their equipment warnings worth knowing: no glass mercury thermometers (they can break and release toxic fumes), and ear thermometers aren't reliable before 6 months.

The American Academy of Pediatrics' fever thresholds by age

These are the Academy's guidance points for when to make contact — and they frame them as "pick up the phone at this number," not "everything below this number is fine." A baby who looks unwell warrants the call regardless of the thermometer, per the same guidance.

Your child's age Academy of Pediatrics threshold Their guidance
Under 3 months 100.4°F (38°C) rectal Emergency care, immediately. No exceptions.
3–6 months 101°F (38.3°C) Call your pediatrician — the American Academy of Pediatrics' symptom guidance says fever at this age should be discussed with a doctor within 24 hours.
6–24 months 103°F (39.4°C) Call your pediatrician. The American Academy of Pediatrics also flags a fever lasting more than 48 hours with no other symptoms in a 6–12 month old.
Over 2 years Behavior over number Per the American Academy of Pediatrics: call if a fever lasts more than 3 days, goes above 104°F (40°C), returns after being gone 24+ hours, or your child seems genuinely unwell.

One boundary note from the American Academy of Pediatrics' own materials: the infant fever guideline runs to 60 days with a cautious approach out to 12 weeks — so a baby anywhere near the 3-month line with a fever is a "call your pediatrician" at minimum.

Is the fever itself dangerous?

The American Academy of Pediatrics' answer: almost never — the fever is the response, not the disease. When the body detects an infection, the brain deliberately raises the temperature to help fight it.

Two things follow from that, both per the American Academy of Pediatrics, and both surprise most parents. A higher number does not mean a sicker child — the temperature says the immune system is engaged, not what it is engaged against. And fever reducers make a child more comfortable but do not treat the illness — so the Academy frames the goal as a comfortable child, not a specific number.

Which is why the American Academy of Pediatrics' guidance keeps returning to the same theme: watch the child, not the thermometer.

The symptoms the American Academy of Pediatrics says matter more than the number

The American Academy of Pediatrics' symptom guidance sorts the red flags roughly like this — and every one of them means making contact, whatever the thermometer says.

Their "call 911 now" tier: severe trouble breathing (struggling for each breath, can barely cry); not moving or cannot be woken; purple or blood-colored spots on the skin; or your own judgment that this is life-threatening.

Their "go to the ER now" tier: any fever of 100.4°F+ in a baby under 3 months; a seizure with the fever; a stiff neck; a bulging soft spot; a child who is hard to wake or "out of it" when awake.

Their "call your doctor now" tier: trouble breathing that isn't severe; real difficulty swallowing; any temperature above 104°F (40°C); shaking chills lasting more than 30 minutes; nonstop crying, or crying whenever touched or moved; not moving an arm or leg normally; signs of dehydration (the American Academy of Pediatrics lists fewer than six wet diapers a day, dry mouth, fewer tears, a sunken soft spot); any child with a weakened immune system.

About the rash in that first tier. The CDC lists a dark purple rash — one that does not fade under pressure — among the signs of meningococcal bloodstream infection, and notes that in babies the illness can also show as poor feeding, unusual sleepiness, irritability, and a bulging soft spot, worsening rapidly after starting out flu-like. It is rare. It is also why the "call now" tier exists.

If any of this list is in front of you right now and it feels like an emergency: 911 first. That is not us — that is what your own pediatrician's line would tell you.

What does the NIH say about febrile seizures?

The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) — the NIH institute that publishes the parent-facing fact sheet on this — describes febrile seizures as seizures triggered by fever, usually as the temperature rises quickly, affecting roughly 2–5% of children, most often between 6 months and 5 years.

Two findings from that literature that parents deserve to hear together: watching one is one of the most frightening things a parent experiences, and a simple febrile seizure — a single episode of 15 minutes or less in 24 hours — is described in the clinical literature as typically harmless, with no link to long-term neurological or cognitive problems. NINDS states plainly that having a febrile seizure does not mean a child has epilepsy.

NINDS's guidance for during a seizure: note the start time; turn the child onto their side; put nothing in their mouth; don't restrain them — move hard objects away instead; stay with them. Their line for 911: a seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes, a child who doesn't recover quickly, or any trouble breathing. And after any first febrile seizure, even a short one: call your pediatrician — they'll want to see your child and find the fever's source.

What does the American Academy of Pediatrics say NOT to do?

All of the following are the American Academy of Pediatrics' published warnings, not ours:

  • No fever medicine for a baby under 12 weeks before evaluation — it can mask the clinical picture.
  • No aspirin for a child or teen with a viral illness — the Academy links it to Reye syndrome, a rare but serious brain and liver condition. That includes anything containing salicylates, so labels on combination products matter.
  • No alcohol baths, ice packs, or cold sponging — no longer recommended and can have adverse effects.
  • No bundling a feverish child in blankets — a single layer is the Academy's guidance.
  • No deciding from touch, fever strips, or a lone armpit reading in a young baby.

On fever reducers and doses: which medicine, whether one is needed at all, and the correct dose are your pediatrician's calls — the American Academy of Pediatrics notes infant and toddler dosing goes by weight, not age. A practical habit many practices suggest: ask for your child's current dose at each well visit and write it down before you need it at 2am.

So when do I call, and who?

Simple version, and it is the same one your pediatrician's office gives:

If you think it's an emergency — call 911. Every after-hours line opens with this sentence, and it means exactly what it says: your judgment that something is seriously wrong is reason enough.

For everything else — call your pediatrician. The thresholds in the table above are the American Academy of Pediatrics' markers for when to pick up the phone; a fever in a baby under 3 months is the one they route straight to emergency care. Most practices run a nurse line all night, nurses answer this exact question constantly, and there is no such thing as calling too soon about a young baby's fever.

Two things worth doing today, before you need them: put the after-hours number in your phone under a name you can find at 3am, and put a working digital thermometer where you can find it in the dark.

The bottom line

The American Academy of Pediatrics' one absolute: under 3 months and 100.4°F rectally — emergency care, immediately. Not a judgment call, and never an overreaction.

For every age after that, their guidance says the fever is a sign the immune system is doing its job, and what matters more is how your child looks, drinks, breathes and responds to you. When in doubt: call your pediatrician. When it feels like an emergency: 911.

And if you're reading this at 3am with a warm baby on your chest — you're already doing the thing that keeps children safe, which is paying attention.
