When Should I Start Potty Training My Child?

By the ParentingAI team · Last updated August 2026

Start when your child shows readiness signs, usually somewhere between 18 months and 3 years — not on a fixed date. The AAP notes that most children in the United States are bowel and bladder trained by 4 years of age. Starting earlier does not reliably finish earlier.

Which is a relief, because everyone in your life has an opinion and at least one of them potty trained their child at 19 months and mentions it often.

How do I know my child is ready?

AAP's readiness signs on HealthyChildren.org include:

  • Staying dry at least 2 hours at a time during the day, or waking dry from naps
  • Showing signs they are about to go — grunting, freezing, squatting, heading for a corner
  • Being able to pull pants up and down
  • Following simple instructions
  • Interest in the toilet, in underwear, or in what everyone else does in the bathroom
  • Being bothered by a dirty diaper

You do not need every sign. You do need a handful, and you need a child who is not actively fighting the idea.

Signs to wait: they have no awareness of having gone, they refuse to sit on the potty, there is a big change underway (new sibling, a move, starting daycare), or the person who wants this most is you. Waiting a month is not a loss.

What age are most kids potty trained?

Most US children are bowel and bladder trained by age 4, per AAP. A prospective cohort of 482 children cited by American Family Physician found 61% toilet trained by 36 months and 98% by 48 months.

If your 3-year-old is not there yet, they are in a very large, very normal group. This is not a measure of intelligence, and it is not a measure of you.

Does the 3-day potty training method work?

The method: clear a long weekend, stay home, go diaper-free, watch closely, move fast to the potty at the first sign, celebrate everything.

Plenty of families like it. It is decisive, it is short, and the intensity suits some kids and some households.

Evidence note. The claim that "most kids get it by day 3" comes from the books and programs selling the method, not from published research. There is no peer-reviewed trial establishing a 3-day success rate. Compare that with the child-oriented, readiness-led approach described in American Family Physician, which does have published outcome data behind it. Use the 3-day method if it fits your family — with the expectation that it may take longer, and that taking longer is not a failure of the child or of you.

If a weekend of full-attention supervision is not possible, the gradual path works too: introduce the potty casually, let them sit clothed and then not, read potty books, praise any interest, apply no pressure, and expect a few weeks to a few months.

My child pees on the potty but will not poop — what is going on?

This is the most common complication in potty training, and most guides skip it entirely.

Some children will use the potty for urine and refuse it for stool. American Family Physician reports stool toileting refusal occurs in up to 20% of children, and about one in four of those cases needs intervention.

The problem is the loop it can start. A child holds stool to avoid the potty. Held stool gets harder and drier. Passing it hurts. Hurting makes them hold it more. Once that cycle is running, it is not a behavior issue you can encourage your way out of, and pressure makes it worse.

Watch for: stools that are large, hard, or painful; fewer bowel movements than usual; a child who crosses their legs, goes stiff, hides, or dances on tiptoe to hold it in; small smears in the underwear (this can be leakage around a backup, not laziness); belly pain; or a child who asks for a diaper only for poop.

What to do: put stool training on pause and let them have the diaper for poop without comment or consequence — this is not backsliding. Keep them sitting comfortably with feet supported on a stool, so they have something to push against. Offer water and fiber-containing foods. And call your pediatrician — this is exactly the kind of thing they treat routinely and effectively, and there are options that work. Ask them about the right approach for your child rather than starting anything from the pharmacy aisle on your own.

Getting ahead of this early is far easier than untangling it later, so an early call is a good call.

Do boys potty train later than girls?

There is a modest average difference, and it is smaller than the internet suggests. American Family Physician reports readiness signs at a median 24 to 26 months in girls versus 29 months in boys, and median age of daytime dryness at 32.5 months for girls and 35 months for boys.

Those are population medians about two to three months apart, not a rule about your child. The AAP's own toilet training guidance does not tell parents to expect boys to train later. Plenty of boys train before plenty of girls.

What if we start and it is not working?

Stop and try again in a few weeks. Pausing costs you nothing. Pushing through resistance tends to produce power struggles, accidents, and stool withholding — the three things that actually make potty training long.

Never punish or shame an accident. Accidents are part of learning, including months after a child seems fully trained.

When should I call the pediatrician?

  • Any sign of constipation or stool withholding, as above
  • Pain, burning, or crying with urination
  • Your child was reliably trained and starts having frequent accidents again
  • Your child is approaching 4 and shows no interest or readiness at all
  • Daytime wetting that continues past age 5 or 6

Nighttime is separate. Staying dry overnight is a physiological milestone that arrives on its own schedule, often much later, and bedwetting into the early school years is common.

The bottom line: follow your child's readiness rather than a calendar, watch for the poop-holding pattern early, and let go of the timeline. This ends. All of them get there.
