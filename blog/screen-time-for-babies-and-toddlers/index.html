How Much Screen Time Should a Child Under 3 Have?

By the ParentingAI team · Last updated August 2026

As of 2026, the AAP no longer sets hourly screen limits as policy. It asks five questions instead — the 5 Cs: Child, Content, Calm, Crowding Out, Communication. Under 1 hour a day remains an optional anchor for toddlers and preschoolers, not a rule.

If you have been carrying quiet guilt about the twenty-five minutes of Bluey that let you cook dinner, you can put it down. The guidance you were measuring yourself against has been replaced, and the replacement is more forgiving and more useful.

What changed in 2026?

In February 2026, the American Academy of Pediatrics published "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents: Policy Statement" (Pediatrics, Vol. 157, Issue 2, e2025075320), with a companion technical report (e2025075321). It supersedes the 2016 recommendations that produced the numbers everyone memorized: avoid screens under 18 months, 1 hour a day for ages 2 to 5.

The core change is the unit of measurement. The 2016 guidance counted hours. The 2026 statement asks about context — what the child is like, what they are watching, whether screens are doing the emotional regulation, and what the screen is displacing. It also shifts a great deal of responsibility off individual families and onto platforms and policymakers, which is why coverage described it as pediatricians calling for system-level change rather than stricter household rules.

Precision matters here, because this is being oversimplified in both directions:

  • ❌ "The AAP scrapped screen limits, so hours do not matter." Not what it says.
  • ❌ "The 1-hour rule for toddlers is still official policy." Also not what it says.
  • ✅ The AAP no longer sets hour limits as policy, and the statement still offers an hours range as an individualized starting point. Its own words: "Time limits might range from <1 hour/d for toddlers and preschoolers to 1 to 2 hours/d or more of entertainment (not school-related) media for school-aged children and teens."

So "under an hour" has moved from a rule you are failing to a number you can start from and adjust.

What are the 5 Cs?

Straight from the policy statement, these are the five questions:

  • Child — Who is your child? What are their strengths, and how do they respond to media?
  • Content — What are they actually interacting with, and is it worth their attention?
  • Calm — Are screens being used to manage emotions or to get to sleep?
  • Crowding Out — What is media getting in the way of? Sleep, play, movement, reading, being with people.
  • Communication — How does your family talk about media?

For a child under 3, Calm and Crowding Out are the two doing the most work.

Calm: if the tablet is the only thing that ends a meltdown, the tantrum skills are not being built. This is not a reason to panic over the occasional restaurant rescue; it is a reason to notice if a screen has become the default off-switch.

Crowding Out: the AAP's emphasis is on prioritizing healthy activities — sleep, play, physical activity, and reading. If those are intact, the number of minutes matters much less.

Are screens bad for babies under 2?

The 2026 statement is measured about this: "Infants do not learn from digital media, but occasionally viewing brief, high-quality videos (eg, Sesame Street) is not detrimental."

That is a meaningful softening of the 2016 framing. Babies do not get educational value from screens — their learning comes from faces, objects, and back-and-forth with people. And an occasional short, good-quality video is not doing harm.

Both halves are true at once. Do not count on a screen to teach your baby anything; do not lie awake over the fact that they watched some of a show while you took a shower.

Do video calls with grandparents count?

They are treated differently, and always have been. The 2026 statement: "Videochatting can provide opportunities for bidirectional interactions for children."

A live call with a person who responds to your baby is a conversation, not consumption. Call the grandparents.

Does watching together actually matter?

Yes, and this is the highest-leverage thing you can do. The AAP: "Joint media engagement is associated with greater child and teen learning. Effects are strengthened by joint media engagement (eg, viewing together, teaching) with a caregiver."

Watching together does not require your full attention for the whole episode. Naming what is on screen, asking a question, connecting it to something real in the room — that is what turns passive minutes into shared ones.

And if you cannot co-view because you are cooking or on a call or out of energy, that is a normal Tuesday, not a lapse. The point is that co-viewing is worth doing when you can, not a standard you are failing between times.

Where is AAP's parent-facing guidance now?

AAP's consumer site, HealthyChildren.org, has been updated and now carries the 5 Cs framework directly, including age-specific question sets for infants and for toddlers and preschoolers. Those pages do not publish an hourly cap.

The 2016 numbers are nonetheless still everywhere — in older articles, daycare handbooks, pediatric office handouts, school newsletters, and the top of a lot of search results. If someone quotes "no screens under 18 months, one hour for ages 2 to 5" at you as current AAP policy, that is the 2016 guidance.

Evidence note. Almost every specific screen-time number you will encounter online — two hours, thirty minutes, none before age 2 — is convention rather than published pediatric guidance. As of the 2026 policy statement, the only figures the AAP itself puts in print for young children are the optional "<1 hour/d for toddlers and preschoolers" range quoted above. If a number is presented to you as a medical threshold, it is fair to ask who published it.

What should I actually do tonight?

A workable version of the 5 Cs for a household with a child under 3:

  1. Pick the content deliberately. Slow, calm, narrative shows over fast-cut autoplay. Turn autoplay off — it is the single highest-yield setting change available to you.
  2. Keep screens out of the sleep routine. Screens close to bedtime and in the bedroom are the most consistent problem in the research.
  3. Watch a little of it with them when you can, and talk about it after.
  4. Notice if screens have become the only calming tool, and keep building the others alongside.
  5. Check what it is displacing. Sleeping enough? Moving? Playing? Being read to? Getting real face time? If yes, the minute count is not your problem.
  6. Pick a rough daily target if that helps you — under an hour is a reasonable place to start for a toddler — and hold it loosely. Sick days, travel days, and days you need to make dinner are not the days to enforce it.

What about older kids?

This post covers children under 3. The 5 Cs apply at every age, but the specifics change a lot for school-age children and change again for teens, where the questions are less about hours and more about social media, sleep, and what is happening in the content. We cover those separately.

The bottom line: you are not damaging your child with screens. The 2026 AAP guidance moved the conversation from counting minutes to asking better questions — and by those questions, most families are already doing fine.
